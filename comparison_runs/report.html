L1 minimum-fuel benchmarks · Hawkins (2021) §4.5

Are the FBSDE solvers competitive with iLQR and MPPI?

DT-FBSDE, FBRRT, box-constrained iLQR, and MPPI on the three L1 minimum-fuel benchmarks, all scored as closed-loop policies on the same 1,024 held-out noisy rollouts. On the two 2-D problems a grid dynamic-programming solution gives the true stochastic optimum, so “gap” there means distance from optimal.

Run on 6 October 2026, 8 cores, one method at a time. 10 seeds on each 2-D problem, 6 on the double inverted pendulum. Branch exp/ilqr-mppi-comparison, built on the uncommitted DT-FBSDE slope-matching work in exp/dt-fbsde-small-batch.

Short answer
  • On the 2-D problems DT-FBSDE is the most accurate method tested, and the only one that reaches the optimum. It ends 0.2% above optimal on the double integrator and 3.2% above on the pendulum, on every seed. iLQR stops at +7.6% and +34%, and MPPI with LQR tracking at +46% and +72%. Both are certainty-equivalent local methods, and that is roughly where they plateau.
  • iLQR is much faster to a rough answer. It reaches its plateau in 0.2 ms on the double integrator and 80 ms on the pendulum. DT-FBSDE needs 0.37 s to get within 5% on the double integrator, and on the pendulum 1.1 s to get within 25% and 17 s to get within 5%. If an answer 30% above optimal is good enough, iLQR wins on speed.
  • On the double inverted pendulum no offline method converges, and the FBSDE methods do worst. DT-FBSDE in its existing configuration is still about 3× the best known cost after 300 s, at about 4.5 s per iteration. Receding-horizon MPPI gets within about 5% of the best policy found, but needs 52 ms of planning per control step.
  • FBRRT is not competitive on any problem. It trails DT-FBSDE everywhere, and trails iLQR on both pendulums.

Recommendation: keep DT-FBSDE as the core and drop FBRRT. DT-FBSDE shows a real advantage that local trajectory optimizers don't have. The open problem is scaling it to four dimensions, and the cheapest next experiment is to seed it with iLQR.

How the comparison works

Every method gets the same budget of charged solver time per run: 60 s on the 2-D problems and 300 s on the double inverted pendulum, with MPPI capped at 120 s there because its curve is flat by then. Only time inside the solver's own iteration counts. At geometrically spaced checkpoints, the method's current policy is rolled out on a fixed set of 1,024 noisy rollouts that no solver samples from, and that held-out cost is the score. Every method, seed and checkpoint is scored on the same noise.

  • DT-FBSDE. On the 2-D problems, the best configuration from the batch sweep in the other session: degree-6 polynomial value functions, slope matching (weight 10 on the double integrator, 1 on the pendulum), batches of 16, half the paths at 6× noise, and the screened, stop-uphill line search. On the double inverted pendulum, the FBRRT comparison’s configuration (soft-min quadratics, 4,096 paths). A quick test of the 2-D recipe on the 4-D problem stalled at costs of 13 and 49, so it was not used.
  • FBRRT. The FBRRT comparison’s defaults.
  • iLQR. Control-limited iLQR (Tassa et al. 2014) on the noiseless dynamics. The L1 cost is smoothed (pseudo-Huber) and the smoothing is shrunk to 10−3. Each seed starts from random controls. The policy is the nominal trajectory tracked by gains from a backward pass at smoothing 1. Gains at the solve’s own smoothing vanish wherever the plan coasts and cost +57% on the double integrator.
  • MPPI. 256 noiseless samples per iteration, temperature set each iteration to give an effective sample size of 5%, and exploration noise decaying from 0.5 to 0.02. It was scored open loop (“MPPI”) and with the same LQR tracking gains (“MPPI+LQR”). Rolling the samples out with the problem’s own noise made it worse.

Gap = held-out cost ÷ reference − 1. The tables count a seed as reaching a band only if its final policy is in it; the time columns show the median time at which a seed first reached the band.

Online replanning

MPC is how iLQR and MPPI are normally deployed, so each was also run as a receding-horizon controller. It starts from that seed’s offline solution and re-plans at every step of 128 of the held-out rollouts: iLQR with 2 iterations per step, MPPI with 2 iterations of 256 samples. This is not an offline policy, and its cost is online compute at every control step. On the 2-D problems the comparison uses the grid optimum on the same 128 rollouts. On the double inverted pendulum it uses the 1,024-rollout reference, which is a looser comparison.

Replanning helps on the double integrator: iLQR-MPC gets within 3.7% at 17 µs per step. It does not help on the pendulum, where both controllers stay 35–45% above optimal, well behind DT-FBSDE’s offline policy. On the double inverted pendulum, MPPI-MPC is the best controller tested (about +5%), at 52 ms per 25 ms control step on one core. iLQR-MPC inherits its plan’s fragility there.

Recommendation

Are the FBSDE methods competitive? DT-FBSDE is more than competitive on the 2-D problems: it is the only method that reaches the stochastic optimum, and it does so reliably. FBRRT is not competitive. On the 4-D problem neither is competitive yet, and that is the result that matters most for where to go next.

Keep the FBSDE theory as the core, and use iLQR to make it scale. The 2-D results show what the FBSDE machinery adds that local methods lack: a value function that captures the noise, so the policy is stochastically optimal and not just a tracked deterministic plan. Starting from iLQR or MPPI and “adding FBSDE” would mean rebuilding that piece anyway. What the 4-D run shows is that DT-FBSDE spends almost all of its budget getting from a cost of 99 down to about 10. iLQR covers that ground in about a second. Concrete next steps, in order:

  1. Seed DT-FBSDE with iLQR. An iLQR backward pass already gives a quadratic value function at every stage, centered on its nominal trajectory, which is exactly one component of the soft-min model. Initialize the value-function table from it, and sample the first forward passes around its nominal trajectory. If DT-FBSDE then refines the double inverted pendulum toward the best known cost, the method scales by borrowing local structure rather than exploring from scratch.
  2. Retune DT-FBSDE in 4-D from that start. The 2-D recipe (small batches, slope matching, mixed exploration) stalled when carried over. It may behave differently from a good warm start.
  3. Drop FBRRT, unless there is a specific hypothesis for why its tree structure should help at higher dimension. Here it is slower and less reliable than DT-FBSDE on every problem.
  4. Possible publishable angle: iLQR’s best policies are found and then lost. Its iterates pass near the best known double-inverted-pendulum cost and then degrade. DT-FBSDE’s rollout line search and value-function fit are the missing ingredient. “iLQR made noise-aware by FBSDE value estimates” is the hybrid to try if the warm start in step 1 is not enough.

A fallback, if 4-D scaling stays out of reach: use a DT-FBSDE value function as the terminal cost of short-horizon MPPI. That keeps the FBSDE contribution and puts the online computation where MPC already works.

Caveats

  • The tuning effort is uneven. DT-FBSDE’s 2-D configuration came from a long sweep against the same grid optimum used here. iLQR and MPPI got a short pilot sweep: the smoothing for iLQR’s gains, and MPPI’s noise, sample count and process-noise setting, chosen on held-out cost. On the 4-D problem the bias runs the other way: DT-FBSDE was not retuned there at all.
  • Wall-clock time depends on implementation. All four methods are C++ on the same machine. iLQR is serial, MPPI and evaluation use all 8 cores, and the FBSDE solvers use what they already did. The orders of magnitude are robust; the small factors are not.
  • The double inverted pendulum has no true optimum. Its gaps are measured against the best policy found, so they understate the distance to optimal.
  • The MPC controllers are scored on 128 rollouts and with only 3 seeds each, so treat their numbers as indicative.
  • The FBSDE code is uncommitted work from another session. If exp/dt-fbsde-small-batch changes, rerun with bazel run -c opt //examples:method_comparison and tools/analyze_method_comparison.py.
