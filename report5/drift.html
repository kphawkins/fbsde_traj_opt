DT-FBSDE · Taylor noiseless estimator · random {−1, 0, 1} drifts

Off-Policy Drift Study

The forward pass already replaced the policy's control with a random one from {−1, 0, 1} with probability ε, but ε decayed to 3% within about 20 iterations, so the Taylor estimator's off-policy term had almost nothing to correct. Here ε stays at a fixed floor, from 0.03 up to 0.5. The question is whether those random drifts, read through the K term, give a better value function and a better policy. Every run uses the degree-4 monomial value function and a 30-second budget, with 3 seeds per setting. The settings that did best in 2-D then ran on the double inverted pendulum.

What the runs show so far
  • Bottom line. With random drift in the batch, the K term is heavily used, and on the nonlinear pendulum removing it costs 5–10 points. But in this noiseless form it reproduces what exact re-evaluation of Ṽ at the policy's landing point already computes, to within about 1%, and exact re-evaluation performs the same. Random drift with that correction can replace noise inflation on the pendulum, a gain of about one point (+4.2% against +5.1%). On the double inverted pendulum it does not beat the old double-greedy default: +58% against +50% at ε → 0.03, and worse at ε = 0.1.
  • Random drift makes the K term substantial. ‖D‖ grows linearly with ε, from 0.06 at ε = 0.03 to about 1.0 at ε = 0.5. Averaged over all samples it is about 1% of the target, because most samples follow the policy. On the samples that took a random drift it averages 1–6 cost units, against a mean target of about 11–12, so on those samples it moves the target by 15–50%.
  • On the pendulum the K term carries real weight. Dropping it (order 0) raises the final gap from +4–5% to +8–10% at ε = 0.1, and from +15–16% to +25% at ε = 0.3, for both target policies. On the double integrator, dropping it does no harm: +0.4% against +0.6% at ε = 0.1, and +1.0% against +8.5% at ε = 0.3.
  • The second-order expansion is accurate. The full estimator's targets sit within about 0.01–0.02 of the exact re-evaluation, about 1% of the correction they apply. Keeping only the first-order term performs about the same as the full estimator.
  • Random drift can replace noise inflation, but only when the target corrects back to the policy. Without the 6× inflation on half the paths, the Taylor estimator of μ at ε = 0.1 reaches +3.6% on the pendulum, the best pendulum result in this study, and exact re-evaluation reaches +4.0%. Double greedy, which has no such correction, falls to +11% to +14% without inflation.
  • More random drift still hurts overall. Every target is best at ε ≈ 0.1, and all of them reach +22% to +26% on the pendulum at ε = 0.5. The exact re-evaluation target, which never uses the K term, degrades just as much, so the cause is where the samples sit: the degree-4 fit spreads over states the policy never visits.
  • Held random arcs (3–6 stages) hurt everywhere, by +5 to +17 points. Girsanov down-weighting is neutral at best.
  • Confining the random drift to some paths does not help. Half the paths at ε = 0.2 performs like all paths at ε = 0.1. A quarter of the paths at ε = 0.4 is worse. Like held arcs, concentrating the randomness on fewer paths hurts.
  • On 8 seeds, the Taylor estimator of μ with random drift in place of noise inflation edges the old default on the pendulum. It reaches +4.2% (worst +5.4%) against +5.1% (worst +6.5%) for double greedy at ε → 0.03. Exact re-evaluation reaches +4.0% (worst +6.2%), which is the same within noise. On the integrator the old default stays slightly ahead, +0.3% against +0.9%, because that problem benefits from the inflated noise.
  • Recommended 2-D setting: the Taylor target estimating μ, ε = 0.1 held constant, and no noise inflation. Taylor with the greedy target policy, ε = 0.1, with inflation, is the alternative that is best on both problems together: +4.9% and +0.4%.
  • On the double inverted pendulum, double greedy stays ahead. With 4 seeds of 240 s, the median gaps to the best known cost are:
    • double greedy, +50% (worst +73%);
    • exact re-evaluation of μ, +53%;
    • Taylor estimating μ at ε → 0.03, +58%;
    • Taylor at ε = 0.1, +67%;
    • Taylor with the K term dropped, +67%.
    Seeds of one setting spread by 30–90 points, so only the ε = 0.1 penalty and double greedy's narrower worst case stand out. ‖D‖ is 2.7–5 here, against 0.2 in 2-D at the same ε.

The estimator and its K term

For a sample at state xi whose forward step took drift Ki, the noiseless estimator's target for the value of a target policy μ is

Ŷi = ℓ(x, μ(x)) + Ṽ(x̄) + gᵀ ΔF + ½ ΔFᵀ H ΔF + ½ tr(Σᵀ H Σ), x̄ = x + K, ΔF = Fμ(x) − K

Here g and H are the gradient and Hessian of the next stage's fitted value function at x̄. The highlighted term is the K term. When the sample followed μ, ΔF is zero and the target is the plain on-policy one. When it took a random drift, the expansion carries the value from where the sample went to where μ would have sent it.

The page calls the other targets exact re-evaluation and double greedy. Exact re-evaluation is ℓ + Ṽ(x + Fμ) + ½ tr(Σᵀ H(x + Fμ) Σ), which evaluates the fitted function directly at μ's landing point. Double greedy is the minimum over controls from two half-tables, the default before this study.

One property shapes how to read the results. In the noiseless form, the Taylor target is the second-order expansion about x + K of exactly the quantity the exact re-evaluation target computes. Ṽ is a closed-form polynomial that can be evaluated anywhere, so the two targets differ only by the Taylor residual, the third- and higher-order terms. A unit test confirms this: with a quadratic Ṽ, the residual is zero to rounding at any ‖D‖.

So the random drifts' information reaches every target through the states they visit. On top of that, the K term contributes two things of its own:

  • it evaluates Ṽ, g and H at x + K, where the next stage's samples actually landed and its fit is supported;
  • with the greedy target policy, it estimates the improved policy's value rather than μ's.

The stage-2 ablation measures both directly. Dropping the K term (order 0) leaves the value of wherever the sample went. Keeping it (order 2) gives the full estimator.

Stage 1: how much random drift?

The forward pass draws a random control from {−1, 0, 1} with probability ε at each stage of each path. The schedule is εj = ε + (1 − ε)·0.8j: it starts fully random and settles to the floor ε within about 20 iterations. Everything else is as in earlier studies: 6× noise inflation on half the paths, slope matching, and the line search on 256 fixed rollouts. There are four targets. Double greedy is the old default. Exact re-evaluation of μ uses no K term. Two Taylor variants estimate μ and the greedy policy respectively.

How much the K term is used

Each iteration reports the K term's size, averaged over every sample and stage of its backward pass. The table averages those reports over a run's iterations and over seeds.

  • ‖D‖ is the Girsanov drift ‖Σ⁻¹(Fμ − K)‖, the thesis's measure of how far off-policy a sample is. The estimator's bias bound grows as exp(‖D‖²/2), so ‖D‖ = 1 costs a factor of about 1.65.
  • K share is mean |gᵀΔF| over mean |Ŷ|: how much of the target the off-policy correction moves.
  • Stage 2 adds four quantities:
    • the fraction of samples that are off-policy;
    • |K| on those samples alone;
    • the signed mean of gᵀΔF;
    • the Taylor residual |Ŷ − exact re-evaluation|.
    Since gᵀΔF ≈ Ṽ(x + Fμ) − Ṽ(x + K), a negative signed mean says the target policy lands where Ṽ is lower than where the random drift went. The correction then lowers those samples' targets to the target policy's value. In every run here it is negative: random controls lead to costlier states, and the K term credits the better choice.

Probes, a quarter of the batch, are never stepped onward, so they are always evaluated on-policy.

Stage 2: ablation and tricks

Stage 2 has four sets of runs:

  • Ablation of the K term at ε = 0.1 and 0.3: order 0 (no K term), order 1 (first-order only) and order 2 (the full estimator), for both target policies, with the residual measured.
  • Held random arcs: a path that draws a random drift keeps it for 3 or 6 stages, so it reaches the states another bang-off-bang policy would reach.
  • Girsanov weighting: each sample's fit weight is multiplied by exp(−κ‖D‖²/2).
  • Random drift in place of noise inflation: the 6× inflation on half the paths is turned off. Stage 3 adds ε = 0.05 and 0.15 to this group.
  • Exploring only some paths (stage 3): random drift on a half or a quarter of the paths, at a higher ε, with the rest always following the policy.

Read the ablation through two columns. |K| where off-policy is the size of the correction. Taylor residual is how far each target sits from the exact re-evaluation. Order 0 is off by the full correction; order 2 is off by about 1% of it. Grey rows are the matching stage-1 baselines. Green marks the best gap in each group. The diagnostics columns are for the pendulum.

Confirmation on 8 seeds

The four leading settings were rerun on 5 new seeds and merged with their 3 earlier seeds. They are the old default; the Taylor target estimating the greedy policy; and, without noise inflation, the Taylor target estimating μ and exact re-evaluation of μ.

Double inverted pendulum

The reference is 2.4928, the lowest held-out cost any method reached in the method comparison (iLQR). No grid optimum exists in 4-D. Every run uses batch 128, which the sigma-point study found faster here than 4096. They use the comparison's DT-FBSDE configuration: no noise inflation and no slope matching.

The first set ran six at a time, so each run got only about 40 iterations in 180 s and none came close to converging. It ranks how fast each setting starts, not where it ends. Within it, three things hold:

  • double greedy is ahead early (+74%), with the μ-evaluating targets, Taylor and exact re-evaluation alike, behind at +106% to +145%;
  • random drift at ε = 0.1 hurts double greedy badly (+203%);
  • 6× noise inflation is worst of all (+458%).

The K term is far larger here in Girsanov units: ‖D‖ is 5.5 at ε = 0.03 and 8.3 at ε = 0.1, against 0.06 and 0.19 in 2-D. The DIP's noise is small next to its control authority, so one random control is a large change of measure. With the greedy target policy, ‖D‖ reaches 23–34, where the exp(‖D‖²/2) bias bound says nothing.

The main comparison ran two at a time, which gave each run 120–260 iterations, with 4 seeds of 240 s. Its five settings are:

  • the old default;
  • the Taylor estimator of μ at ε = 0.03 and at 0.1;
  • exact re-evaluation of μ;
  • the order-0 ablation.

With the extra iterations the targets come much closer together, and the old default still leads. The K term's ablation does not separate from the full estimator here, because at ε → 0.03 few samples are off-policy. The order-0 runs also got about twice as many iterations, for reasons not yet examined. The DIP needs more than 4 minutes for any of these settings: the best run ends at 3.13 against a reference of 2.49.

How to read the numbers

  • The gap is the policy's cost on 1,024 held-out rollouts relative to the grid dynamic-programming optimum on the same rollouts. The grid value runs about 1% high, so small negative gaps are possible.
  • Each setting has 3 seeds. On the pendulum, seeds of one setting spread by 1–6 points, so differences under about 2 points are noise.
  • Runs stop at 30 seconds of solver time, with 5–6 runs sharing 8 cores, so all settings face the same contention.
  • On the double inverted pendulum the gap is relative to 2.4928, the lowest held-out cost any method reached in the method comparison. It is not a proven optimum.
