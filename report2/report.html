DT-FBSDE · value-function models and sigma-point sampling

Which value model, and do sigma points help?

A literature search for value-function models and for ways to enrich an unscented transform, then experiments on the three L1 benchmarks. Every model and sampler was judged by what it does inside DT-FBSDE: the held-out cost of the policy it produces, how fast it gets there, and how often it breaks the method.

Runs of 6–7 October 2026 on 8 cores, one experiment at a time. Gaps on the 2-D problems are against the grid dynamic-programming optimum on the same 1,024 held-out rollouts. Branch exp/ilqr-mppi-comparison, worktree ~/dev/fbsde_traj_opt-ilqr-mppi.

Short answers
  • Use a degree-4 monomial polynomial, or keep degree 6 on the 2-D problems. At 8 seeds and 60 s on the pendulum they nearly tie: degree 6 ends slightly closer to optimal (median +3.2%, worst seed +4.0%) and degree 4 slightly further (+3.6%, worst +4.6%). Degree 4 gets every seed within 5% twice as fast (12.8 s against 25.7 s) at 30% less time per iteration. On the double inverted pendulum, fitted in closed form, degree 4 reaches a median held-out cost of 4.6 in 180 s, against 8.8 for the soft-min model DT-FBSDE has used there. With the batch cut from 4,096 to 128 it reaches 3.0. That is 21% above the best policy any method has found (2.49), ahead of offline MPPI with LQR tracking (4.4), and the best DT-FBSDE result on this problem so far.
  • No new model family clearly beat the polynomials. Gaussian RBFs on a 5×5 grid have the best pendulum median (+2.8%) but a worse worst seed (+6.5%), take 3× the time per iteration and are about 4× slower to evaluate online. Finer RBFs and splines, Legendre polynomials, soft-min quadratics and a small neural network all did worse, and several made DT-FBSDE break down.
  • The ridge acts as the prior, and it matters more than capacity. Each fit sees 8 samples against 15–66 coefficients. The basis and its regularization decide which of the many exact fits is chosen. Monomials with the default ridge happen to encode a good smoothness prior. An orthonormal basis with the same span loses it and fails.
  • Deterministic sigma points stall DT-FBSDE on the 2-D problems. The UT ends at +48% on the pendulum against +3.6% for random paths. They are perfectly reproducible because they stall at the same point. Rotating the points at random every iteration recovers much of the loss (+19%), which shows that determinism, more than the point structure, is what hurts. On the double inverted pendulum the UT with a degree-4 polynomial looked competitive (median 3.97 against 4.63 for random batches of 4,096). A follow-up traced that to its small batch: random paths at the same batch of 128 do better still (3.01).
Part 1 · Value-function models

What the literature offers, and what fits DT-FBSDE

DT-FBSDE puts two hard requirements on a value-function model. The Taylor Q-function policy needs every model to give a state gradient and Hessian. The line search blends old and new fits coefficient by coefficient, so a fixed parameterization is also needed. A closed-form least-squares fit that can also match slopes is a large practical advantage: no learning rate, no epochs, and one small solve per stage. Capacity: fitting the true value function

The first experiment fits each model, at every stage, directly to the grid optimum's value function and slopes. The states come from the grid policy's rollouts, half at the problem's noise and half at 6×, and the fits use DT-FBSDE's sample weights. The policy that table induces is then scored. This measures a model's capacity and its online cost.

Capacity rises steadily with size. Degree-10 Legendre polynomials, 9×9 RBFs and 7-interval splines come within 2–4% on the double integrator. But even the best fit only gets within 9% on the pendulum, while DT-FBSDE itself reaches 3.6% with a degree-4 polynomial. DT-FBSDE fits targets that are consistent with how its Taylor policy reads the next stage's value function, and that beats fitting the true value function. So fitting the grid's value function is a poor predictor of what a model does inside DT-FBSDE. The remaining sections judge models only by their DT-FBSDE runs. The last column is the online cost of one policy decision.

The dots are each seed's final gap. A cross is a run in which the model made DT-FBSDE break down: the least-squares solve became singular after the fits diverged.

The 8-seed confirmation below refines this ranking.
  • Soft-min components. Two, four and eight quadratics end at +89%, +35% and +22% on the pendulum, with worst seeds of +544%, +103% and +45%. More components help, but the SGD-fitted model stays erratic. Changing the learning rate or epochs did not fix it. On the double integrator they reach 1–2%.
  • Local bases. RBFs with 5 centers per axis (+4.1%) and 5-interval splines (+4.8%) are competitive. With 7 or 9 centers or 7 intervals they degrade to +13–37%, because each half-batch of 8 samples cannot pin down local coefficients. Anchoring the ridge to the previous fit made every local model worse.
  • Neural network. 6–9% on the double integrator, and a failure on the pendulum (+3,000%).

Why the basis matters: the ridge is the prior

The surprise of the screen is that Legendre polynomials, which span exactly the same functions as the monomials of the same degree, are far worse. At degree 6 they reach +47% on the pendulum against +5.4%, and at degrees 8 and 10 they break DT-FBSDE on every pendulum seed. The oracle shows they have the better fit when data are plentiful.

The explanation is the sample count. Double estimation splits the batch of 16 into two halves, so each stage's fit sees 8 values and 8 slopes against 15 to 66 coefficients. The problem is underdetermined, and the ridge (1e-6 per sample) picks the solution. In the monomial basis, a high power such as z⁸ is tiny inside the normalized box. Using it needs a large, heavily penalized coefficient, so the ridge quietly prefers low-degree, smooth fits. An orthonormal basis gives every function unit size, the preference vanishes, and the fit oscillates through the 8 points.

Two tests support this. Adding an explicit curvature (roughness) penalty to the Legendre fit rescues degree 6 (from +47% to +4.7%) but not degrees 8 and 10. And changing the monomial ridge from 1e-6 hurts in both directions: 1e-8 gives +18.5%, 1e-4 +29.8%, and 1e-2 over +1,000% on the pendulum. The current ridge is a sharp optimum, found by accident of the basis rather than by design.

What this means for DT-FBSDE. With a small batch, DT-FBSDE behaves like stochastic approximation. Each iteration fits a heavily regularized model to a handful of fresh samples, and the blend plus line search average those noisy fits over many iterations. The model's job is then less "represent the value function" than "give a stable, smooth update from 8 points." A deliberately designed prior is the principled version of what the monomial ridge does by accident. Two candidates: Bayesian least squares with a degree-weighted prior, or recursive least squares that carries the normal equations across iterations. Either could let higher-capacity models work.

Inside DT-FBSDE: the double inverted pendulum

On the 4-D problem every model ran with the configuration DT-FBSDE has used there (4,096 samples, no slope matching, no inflation), for 180 s of solver time on 3 seeds. The best known cost is 2.49, the best policy any method has produced. Closed-form polynomial fits are the clear winners.

  • Degree 4 reaches a median of 4.6 (seeds 3.1, 4.6, 6.1).
  • Degree 2 reaches 6.0 (4.7–6.2).
  • Soft-min 4, the model used so far, reaches 8.8 (7.4–15.9). In the earlier method comparison it was still at 7.6 after 300 s.
  • Everything else is worse: degree 6 (6.5), soft-min 2 (6.6) and 8 (15.3), RBFs (12.5), splines (16.3), Legendre (13.8, one seed at 55), and the network (9.0).

Degree 4 at 180 s already matches offline MPPI with LQR tracking from the method comparison (4.4) and beats iLQR's final policies (median 9.2). The sigma-point follow-up below then found that the same model with random batches of 128 instead of 4,096 reaches a median of 3.01 (seeds 2.98, 3.01, 3.79), because its cheap iterations let it take 5× as many steps. That is the best DT-FBSDE result on this problem, 21% above the best known policy, with receding-horizon MPPI (about 2.6) the only controller still clearly ahead. The rest of the 2-D recipe (slope matching, inflation) and seeding from iLQR are still untried with this model in 4-D.

Confirmation: the finalists at 8 seeds and 60 s

The seven finalists ran for 8 seeds of 60 s on both 2-D problems, the same budget as the method comparison.

  • Double integrator: every finalist ends within 0.4% of optimal on every seed. They differ only in speed. Degree 2 gets within 5% in 0.07 s, degree 4 in 0.19 s, degree 6 in 0.34 s, and the RBF and spline models in 1–3 s.
  • Pendulum:
    • RBF 5×5: best median (+2.8%), but a worst seed of +6.5% and 3× the time per iteration.
    • Degree 6: +3.2% (worst +4.0%), with all 8 seeds within 5% after a median 25.7 s.
    • Degree 4: +3.6% (worst +4.6%), with all 8 seeds within 5% after a median 12.8 s, twice as fast.
    • Legendre degree 6 with the roughness penalty: +4.2%.
    • Spline 5: +4.4%, and half its seeds never got within 5%.
    • Degree 2: +4.6%, worst +10.1%.
    • Degree 8: broke DT-FBSDE on 1 of 8 seeds.

Degrees 4 and 6 are a near-tie on accuracy. Degree 4 is the faster and cheaper of the two, and degree 6 ends marginally closer. In 4-D degree 4 is clearly better than degree 6 (median 4.6 against 6.5).

Part 2 · Sigma-point sampling

An unscented forward pass for DT-FBSDE

Ways to enrich a sigma-point set, from the literature:

  • Julier's scaled UT sets how far the 2n+1 points spread.
  • Julier's spherical simplex and the minimal sets in Menegaz et al.'s systematization use fewer points.
  • Higher-degree rules add points: the fifth-degree cubature rule of the high-degree cubature Kalman filter (2n²+1 points), and the conjugate unscented transform, whose symmetric sets match moments up to order 8 with far fewer points than Gauss–Hermite grids.
  • Tensor Gauss–Hermite quadrature has 3ⁿ or 5ⁿ points, and its sparse-grid reduction cuts that count.
  • The generalized UT matches skewness and kurtosis with 2n+1 points.
  • The randomized UT of the stochastic integration filter (Duník, Straka & Šimandl, 2013) rotates the set at random.
  • Fourier–Hermite dynamic programming is the closest prior use of sigma points inside dynamic programming.

The implementation (option sigma_point_rule of DtFbsdeIterativeSolver):

  • States at each stage: the stage's state distribution is a Gaussian N(m, LLᵀ), or two: one at the problem's noise and one at 6× it, mirroring today's mixed exploration. The stage's states are the rule's points m + Lξⱼ.
  • Moment propagation: the points are stepped through the closed-loop, noise-free dynamics. The next stage's mean and covariance are their weighted moments plus the process noise covariance, as in a sigma-point filter's prediction step.
  • Probes: every point also gets one-step branches under each control in {−1, 0, 1}. These are the random pass's probes, made deterministic.
  • Double estimation: the second half-batch sees the same rule turned by a fixed rotation, so the two independent fits see different states.
  • Unchanged: the backward pass, the policy, and the line search, which still scores every candidate on random rollouts.

Rules tested:

  • UT: 2n+1 points, the minimal set.
  • Fifth-degree cubature: 2n²+1 points.
  • CUT-style set: center, axes and every corner (2n+2ⁿ+1 points). Its weights and radii were derived here, exact to fifth order and matching the sixth marginal moment, not copied from the paper's tables.
  • Gauss–Hermite grids: 3ⁿ and 5ⁿ points.

Unit tests check each rule's moments, and that the forward pass is identical under different seeds.

Results on the 2-D problems

Each sampler ran with the degree-4 polynomial and the 2-D settings above, on 5 seeds of 30 s. The sigma-point batch holds every state once per half: 80 states for the UT and up to 400 for 5ⁿ Gauss–Hermite. To separate "more states" from "structured states", random paths were also run at matched batches of 80 and 144. The randomly rotated variants draw a fresh orthogonal rotation of the point set every iteration (the randomized UT of the stochastic integration filter).

Your four questions about sigma points

Is there an advantage in computational efficiency? Not on the 2-D problems. The forward pass is a small share of an iteration either way (7–13% with sigma points, about 11% with random paths). What a sigma-point iteration does is fit 80–400 states instead of 16, so the backward pass grows to 46–78% of the time. An iteration costs 1.4–4.6× as much. On the double inverted pendulum the comparison flips: a UT batch of 128 states is 30× smaller than the 4,096 random samples DT-FBSDE uses there, so iterations are about 4× cheaper (see below).

Is it more accurate? The value estimate is, the policy is not. On the double integrator, DT-FBSDE's own estimate of the optimal cost V₀(x₀) is within 6% of the grid's with the UT, against 148% with random batches of 16. On the pendulum it is within 21% against 59%. Yet the UT's policy is +6% and +48% above optimal where random sampling's is +0.3% and +3.6%. Random paths at a matched batch of 80 show the same pattern: accurate value levels (5% and 2%), worse policies (+3.2% and +15%). Accuracy of the value function's level and quality of the policy are different targets, and DT-FBSDE's small random batches trade the first for the second.

Does it still converge to the optimum? Not with a deterministic rule. Deterministic runs accept only 0.2–0.6% of their updates, against 1.7–3.4% for random paths. Once the policy settles, a deterministic forward pass places the states in the same spots every iteration. The fits stop changing, and the line search rejects the same update again and again: a fixed point that is not the optimum. Richer rules make it worse (Gauss–Hermite 3ⁿ +204%, the CUT-style set +506% on the pendulum). Probes are essential: without them the UT ends at +40% and +71%.

Rotating the points at random every iteration tests whether determinism is the cause, and largely confirms it. The randomized UT accepts 0.9–2.7% of updates and recovers much of the loss: +3.4% on the double integrator (from +6.2%) and +19% on the pendulum (from +48%). That is about where random paths with the same batch of 80 end (+3.2% and +15%). What remains of the gap to random batches of 16 is the batch size, not the structure: bigger batches mean fewer iterations in the budget. Randomized higher-degree rules improve too (+85% to +125% on the pendulum, from +204% to +506%) but stay erratic.

Is it more reliable because the noise model is deterministic? In the narrow sense, yes. Most deterministic configurations end at exactly the same cost on every seed (spread 0.0 points), since only the line search's rollouts still depend on the seed. But they reliably converge to the wrong answer, and random sampling's spread is small too (2–3 points on the pendulum) around a far better answer. One deterministic configuration was less reliable: with only the problem-noise Gaussian, the UT spread over 206 points on the pendulum.

Sigma points on the double inverted pendulum

The same comparison on the 4-D problem ran with the soft-min model and the configuration DT-FBSDE has used there (4,096 random samples), and with the degree-4 polynomial. Each configuration had 3 seeds of 180 s.

  • With soft-min quadratics, every sigma-point rule fails (median costs 31–79, against 8.8 for random samples). The SGD-fitted model cannot use the few distinct states it is given.
  • With the degree-4 polynomial, the deterministic UT is competitive. It ends at 3.97, 5.30 and 3.97 against 3.13, 4.63 and 6.07 for random batches of 4,096. Its batch of 128 states makes an iteration about 4× cheaper, so it takes 176 iterations to the random batch's 44. Two of its three seeds end at exactly the same cost: the forward pass is identical, and only the line search's rollouts differ.
  • A follow-up separated the batch size from the structure, with the degree-4 polynomial. Random paths at the same batch of 128 end at 2.98, 3.01 and 3.79 (median 3.01), better than the UT. The randomized UT ends at 4.08, 6.04 and 6.57. So the UT's advantage over 4,096 random samples was the small batch, not the sigma points. The randomized UT's worse result, despite accepting as many updates (26–35%) as the random batch, suggests that in 4-D a 9-point rule spread over a rotating frame covers the state distribution less well than 128 independent samples.

Recommendations

  1. Make the degree-4 monomial polynomial DT-FBSDE's default model, with the ridge at 1e-6. On the 2-D problems it ties degree 6 and is twice as fast to within 5%. In 4-D, fit it in closed form instead of SGD-fitting soft-min quadratics; that alone roughly halved the double inverted pendulum's cost.
  2. Retune the double inverted pendulum around that model, starting from batch 128. With a closed-form fit, small batches win there as they do in 2-D: 3.01 against 4.63 at 4,096. Sweep the batch (32–512), then add slope matching, inflation, and iLQR seeding (from the method comparison). DT-FBSDE is now 21% from the best known policy on this problem, against about 200% in the method comparison.
  3. Treat the fit's prior as a design choice. Each iteration's fit is underdetermined, so the regularizer decides the answer. A degree-weighted Bayesian prior, or recursive least squares carried across iterations, is the principled way to get what the monomial ridge gives by accident. It might let higher-capacity models help instead of break.
  4. Drop soft-min quadratics and neural networks for DT-FBSDE at these dimensions. SGD-fitted models were slower and erratic, and they cannot use a small batch.
  5. Keep random paths for the forward pass, at least on the 2-D problems. Deterministic sigma points stall DT-FBSDE, and randomizing their rotation only brings them back to what random paths of the same batch size do. In 4-D the UT's apparent advantage came from its small batch: random paths at the same batch do better. Sigma points' one clear advantage is the estimate of the expected cost, so they remain useful to report V₀(x₀).

Method notes and caveats

  • Few seeds in places. The 2-D screens use 3 seeds of 30 s, the double-inverted-pendulum studies 3 seeds of 180 s, and the sigma-point study 5 seeds. The 8-seed confirmations settle the 2-D ranking; the 4-D results, with 3 seeds, are indicative only.
  • Uneven tuning. The 2-D DT-FBSDE settings were tuned (by ff) for the degree-6 polynomial. Other models ran with them unchanged, apart from the hyperparameter sweeps shown. Soft-min and network models had small learning-rate and epoch sweeps.
  • Oracle targets are approximations. They come from the grid's bilinear interpolation, with slopes by finite differences at the cell size.
  • Uncertain screen entries. In the first model round, two configurations ran while I was compiling on the same machine. The affected timings are flagged in the logs and do not change any conclusion. The roughness-penalty runs of round 2 used a binary built before the option existed. They were discarded and rerun.
  • The sigma-point option touches uncommitted code. It modifies dt_fbsde_iterative_solver.hpp, which is the other session's uncommitted file, but only in this worktree's copy. It is off by default and changes nothing otherwise.
  • Reproducing: bazel run -c opt //examples:value_model_study (phases oracle and dt), //examples:sigma_point_study, and the scripts tools/analyze_value_model_study.py and tools/analyze_sigma_point_runs.py.
